Last winter, from December to March, we packed up our off-grid camper Humbär and headed south to Greece. It didn't take long for Greece to become our all-time favorite country for vanlife. In this post we're sharing the whole story, from the people we met to the adventures we stumbled into along the way. ## Where did we go? Our route through Greece

![sunrise with a sea view from the van](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/sunrise-with-a-sea-view.webp#left)
*When the stars align, you wake up to this: sunrise, sea view, pure picture-book vanlife*

We caught the ferry from Ancona, Italy, to Igoumenitsa, Greece, and from there we meandered our way down toward Patras and over onto the Peloponnese.

The original plan? Take it easy along the west coast and explore every single "finger" of the Peloponnese. The west coast had other ideas, though, and served up a whole string of rainy days. So we changed course, drove east all the way to Corinth, and slowly worked our way south from there. That's no coincidence: the western side of the Peloponnese soaks up most of the winter rain, while the Taygetos and Parnon mountain ranges keep the east tucked away in their rain shadow. So if you're hunting for winter sunshine, heading east is usually a safe bet.

Here's roughly how the route played out:

## What's the weather really like in Greece in winter?

![candles in the van on a rainy day](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/rainy-days-by-candlelight.webp#right)
*Those happen too: cozy rainy days with candlelight and the sound of the waves*

Honestly? A lot chillier than we'd expected.

We'd pictured balmy 20 °C (68 °F) days and endless sunshine. What we mostly got was 10 to 15 °C (50 to 59 °F), plus more than our fair share of rain. And that's pretty much what the climate data says: in the southern Peloponnese, daytime highs in January average around 14 to 15 °C (57 to 59 °F), and nights dip to more like 5 to 7 °C (41 to 45 °F). Winter is also Greece's true rainy season, with the bulk of the year's rain coming down between November and February.

![a sunset over the sea](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/sunsets-like-this.webp#left)
*But when it all came together, we got sunsets like this*

The weather ended up calling a lot of the shots. More than once, we planned our route around wherever the forecast looked friendliest, and the east side generally felt a few degrees kinder.

Still, our verdict is a happy one: the good days easily outnumbered the gloomy ones. And when the sun did come out, winter in Greece felt every bit as dreamy as we'd imagined.

## Can you really live off-grid in a van through a Greek winter?

![reading in the warm van in the evening](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/reading-in-the-warm-van.webp#left)
*Curling up with a good book in our cozy, warm van*

Short answer: yes, but not forever.

We live gas-free and run everything on a powerful electrical system instead: 5 kWh of battery capacity and 500 Wp of solar on the roof. If you'd like the details, check out our post on [Humbär's electrics](/journal/vanlife-without-gas-camper-electrics/).

During the Greek winter, depending on the weather, we had to track down an outlet roughly every five to seven days to fully recharge our battery. Even on clear days, the winter sun hangs low in the sky, so our panels produce far less energy than in summer. To put some numbers on it: at the latitude of Athens, the midday sun around the winter solstice climbs only about 29 degrees above the horizon, compared with more than 75 degrees in June. On top of that, you get just 9.5 hours or so of daylight instead of around 14. Panels lying flat on the roof lose a big chunk of their output, which is why a tilting panel or a portable add-on panel really pays off in winter.

So for us, living off-grid never meant doing without shore power entirely. It just meant we only needed it every now and then.

And the second the sun disappeared, the temperature dropped fast. That's when our wood stove earned its keep, crackling away in the evenings and early mornings.

Lots of campgrounds shut down for the winter, and most beach towns turn into near ghost towns once the season wraps up. So sometimes wild camping was simply the only option that made sense. And in the vast majority of cases, nobody batted an eye at us parking overnight. If anything, we got the sense that locals were happy to see anyone at all in the off-season.

## How strictly is Greece's wild camping ban actually enforced?

![the dream spot by the sea](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/the-dream-spot-by-the-sea.webp#left)
*The dream spot by the sea*

Quick disclaimer: this is just our personal experience, not legal advice. Whenever we wild camped, we never had any trouble with the local police. We always behaved respectfully, were clearly just parked, and never set up camp.

Now for the legal side: wild camping is generally banned in Greece, and since January 2025 the stricter Law 5170/2025 has been in effect. It prohibits parking motorhomes and pitching tents on beaches and coastal strips, at archaeological sites, in forests, and in public spaces in general for longer than 24 hours. Campgrounds and official municipal parking areas are allowed. Fines are reportedly €300 per person, and if it goes to court, you could be looking at up to €3,000 or as much as three months in jail. The key is knowing the difference between parking and camping: a table, chairs, an awning and a campfire turn a parked vehicle into a camp.

At one beach, some people came over to let us know that the police were about to clear the place out, since quite a crowd of campers had gathered there by then. At another beach, a local let us know that overnight stays weren't allowed. Both times, packing up and moving on was the obvious thing to do.

We were also super careful to only stay where there were no "no camping" signs, and to always leave our spot clean and unobtrusive.

## What's the camper infrastructure like in Greece?

### Toilets, showers, trash, laundry and supermarkets

![an outdoor shower next to the van](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/outdoor-shower.webp#right)

We'd gotten spoiled by the great service infrastructure in Spain, France and Norway, where municipal RV areas for dumping gray and black water and filling up on fresh water are all over the place. In Greece? Not so much. Anything like that is few and far between.

### Wildlife favorites: tortoises

![a tortoise in a park in athens](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/tortoise-in-athens.webp#right)
*A truly special encounter: a tortoise right in Athens*

If luck is on your side, you've got a real shot at spotting tortoises in Greece. They wake up from hibernation starting in March. We were lucky enough to admire these majestic animals in the parks of Athens, and if the stars align, you might also cross paths with one on the hiking trails of the Peloponnese. As with any wild animal, keep your distance and give them the respect they deserve. Three species live in Greece: the Hermann's tortoise, the spur-thighed tortoise and the marginated tortoise. All three are strictly protected. Touching them, moving them, or even taking one home is illegal and can be dangerous for the animals: they stick to a fixed territory and rarely find their way back once they've been relocated.

### What caught us off guard: some things were surprisingly pricey

![a road winding through the greek landscape](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/driving-in-greece.webp#left)
*Driving in Greece. Sometimes the views leave you speechless. Sometimes, it's the cost of driving toll roads*

**Groceries at the supermarket**

Believe it or not, some groceries cost more than in Germany, and a few even beat Swiss prices. For certain products, we'd definitely expected a much smaller bill.

**Gas**

Note: our numbers are from before the Iran war. Back then, we never filled up for less than about €1.65 per liter (roughly $7 per gallon).

**Highway tolls**

Tolls were hands down the most painful expense. On fairly short stretches of highway, our 2.6-meter-tall (8.5 ft) motorhome sometimes was charged anywhere from €10 to €25. It comes down to vehicle categories: most Greek toll operators draw the line at an overall height of 2.20 meters (7.2 ft). Anything taller gets bumped from category 2 to category 3 and pays a whole lot more, sometimes double.

That said, a lot of the alternatives meant winding mountain roads in rather questionable shape, so we were still grateful for the smooth highways. Whenever we had time to spare, though, we happily skipped the tolls and took the scenic route through gorgeous villages and quiet country roads. Good to know: Greek toll booths sometimes pop up out of nowhere in the middle of a stretch that's been free up to that point. Paying by card was never an issue at any of them, and every once in a while they even took AmEx.

## Can you swim in the sea in Greece in winter?

### Vanlife favorites: hot springs

From Methana all the way to Thermopylae, the ultimate highlight is sulfury, bubbling hot water gushing straight out of the ground. Sure, you won't come out any cleaner, but on a cold, windy day, sinking into one of nature's free hot tubs at over 36 °C (97 °F) feels stinkingly divine. What a wonder this planet is! A practical tip: silver jewelry tarnishes within minutes in the sulfur water, so take it off before you hop in.

## What's the vanlife community like in Greece in winter?

![friends together in chalkidiki](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/chalkidiki-full-of-life.webp#right)
*With friends, sleepy, deserted Chalkidiki suddenly came alive*

Nowhere else have we had so many spontaneous, heartwarming encounters with fellow vanlifers as in Greece.

The weather basically herded everyone into the same few regions and routes. So we kept bumping into the same faces in different places, made a bunch of new friends and had a wonderful time together.

![friends in chalkidiki](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/friends-in-chalkidiki.webp)
*Good times with friends in Chalkidiki*

![a beach in chalkidiki](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/chalkidiki-days.webp)

![movie night in chalkidiki](/images/journal/overwintering-in-greece/movie-night-in-chalkidiki.webp)
*Movie night in Chalkidiki*

Since we were traveling in the off-season, our neighbors were usually a mix of snowbird retirees and fellow vanlifers. The big sights were way quieter, too: outside of Athens, it was mostly RV travelers and locals.

And maybe that was the most beautiful part of a Greek winter: emptier roads, sleepier towns, and a vanlife community that somehow felt closer than ever.

## Would we do a vanlife winter in Greece again?

Hell yeah, in a heartbeat! Sure, the cold, the rain and the sometimes nonexistent camper infrastructure threw us a few curveballs. But they pale next to everything we got in return: dreamy beaches, Greek food we still daydream about, heartwarming encounters and wildly diverse nature. We'd come back any day.
